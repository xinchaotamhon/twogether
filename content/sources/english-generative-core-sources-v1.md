# English Generative Core v1 — evidence and scope

This file is the project-owned source map for the 80-card English foundation. It does not copy the owner conversation or any external publication. It records which sources support the curriculum model, the limits applied to those sources, and where Human review is still required.

## Grammar backbone

- Rodney Huddleston, *English Grammar: An Outline*, “The structure of kernel clauses”: <https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139166003.005>. Supports the subject/predicate, verb-headed VP, complement, and clause-structure backbone.
- Rodney Huddleston, “Tense, aspect and modality”: <https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139166003.006>. Supports treating tense, aspect, and modality as related but distinct systems and examining present/past uses beyond simple timeline labels.
- Huddleston, Pullum, Payne et al., *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*, “Nouns and noun phrases”: <https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316423530.006>. Supports noun-phrase structure and reference as a first-class foundation rather than an optional branch.
- Huddleston, Pullum, and Reynolds, *A Student's Introduction to English Grammar*, 2nd edition: <https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009085748>. Its chapter architecture supports separate treatment of complements, determinatives, adjuncts, negation, clause type, subordination, relatives, coordination, and information structure.
- *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* contents and chapter map: <https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-grammar-of-the-english-language/A78402ABF5176AD283494180BCA2046F>. Used as a coverage check, not as permission to reproduce the book.

The cards teach a learner-facing descriptive model, not one mandatory linguistic theory. Terms are included only when they help Hiệp or Hoàng predict, explain, contrast, or produce a fresh sentence.

## Speech, rhythm, and listening boundary

- Amalia Arvaniti, “Rhythm, timing and the timing of rhythm”: <https://doi.org/10.1159/000208930>. Supports rejecting a rigid stress-timed/syllable-timed split and focusing instead on grouping, prominence, and timing evidence.

The curriculum therefore teaches prominence, weak forms, schwa, linking, segmentation, and intonation as perceptual/production mechanisms. It does not promise that imitating reductions alone creates fluent speech, and it does not make unsupported claims about Vietnamese cognition.

## Vocabulary, collocation, and phrasal-verb boundary

- Paul Nation, “How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening?”: <https://doi.org/10.3138/cmlr.63.1.59>. Supports frequency-informed prioritization while showing that unassisted coverage depends on medium and requires far more than a magical first thousand items.
- Narges Mahpeykar and Andrea Tyler, “A principled Cognitive Linguistics account of English phrasal verbs with up and out”: <https://doi.org/10.1017/langcog.2014.15>. Supports a continuum from compositional spatial/metaphorical patterns to learned conventional meanings.
- Frank Boers and Stuart Webb, “Teaching and learning collocation in adult second and foreign language learning”: <https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444817000301>. Supports treating collocation as recurrent/restricted co-occurrence rather than arbitrary word lists alone.
- Satoshi Yamagata, Tatsuya Nakata, and James Rogers, “Effects of distributed practice on the acquisition of verb–noun collocations”: <https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263122000225>. Supports spaced encounters with reusable node words and testing transfer to unstudied combinations.

The cards avoid claiming every collocation can be deduced. Learners use semantic patterns to make hypotheses, then confirm conventional combinations through trustworthy examples and real input.

## Learning method

- Jeffrey Karpicke and Henry Roediger, “The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning”: <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408>. Supports retrieval before feedback rather than repeated passive study.
- Nicholas Cepeda et al., “Spacing Effects in Learning”: <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x>. Supports distributed review while showing that a useful gap depends on the intended retention interval.

These studies support the app's attempt-before-reveal and FSRS-backed spacing, but they do not prove that a self-graded English card establishes pronunciation, transfer, or communicative success. Production tasks and the seven-day Human pilot remain required.

## Review boundary

All 80 cards are AI-authored drafts. Human review must still check whether each Vietnamese explanation is clear, each English example is natural for the intended variety/context, and each self-grade target is bounded enough for an honest `Nhớ`/`Quên` decision.
